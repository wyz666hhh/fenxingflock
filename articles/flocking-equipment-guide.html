Flocking Equipment Guide: How to Choose an Electrostatic Flocking Machine

Published: August 17, 2026 | By Fenxing Flock Team

The best fiber and adhesive still produce a poor flocked surface if the equipment isn't right. Flocking machines control the electrostatic field that makes fibers stand on end and anchor into the adhesive — and small differences in voltage, feed rate, and grounding change the final density and hand feel. This guide walks through the equipment types, the components that matter, and how to match a machine to your application.

Most shops start with a manual or semi-automatic setup and add an automatic line as order volume grows. The equipment you choose also determines which fiber lengths and densities you can realistically achieve.

How an Electrostatic Flocking Machine Works

Electrostatic flocking uses a high-voltage DC field to orient and accelerate fibers. The adhesive-coated substrate is grounded, and the machine charges the fibers so they fly toward the surface and embed vertically, like the bristles of a brush. That vertical orientation is what gives flocking its soft, dense, velvet-like feel. Key variables:

  • Voltage — typically 30–120 kV depending on fiber length and density; higher voltage drives longer fibers deeper.
  • Field distance — the gap between the charging electrode and the substrate, which affects fiber orientation and penetration.
  • Feed rate — how fast fiber is metered into the field, which sets coating density and uniformity.
  • Grounding — a weak or uneven ground produces patchy, low-density flocking.

Equipment and Materials Must Match

A machine doesn't work in isolation — it has to suit your fiber length, adhesive, and substrate. Long fibers need higher voltage to stand upright; thick adhesives need more open time to keep fibers anchored; conductive substrates flock more evenly than insulators. Buying equipment and materials from separate vendors without coordination is the most common cause of a patchy, low-density result.

  • Match fiber length to voltage range — confirm the machine can drive your longest fiber.
  • Match adhesive open time to line speed — the adhesive must still be wet when fibers land.
  • Pre-treat insulating substrates — otherwise static build-up causes uneven coverage.
  • Test on your real part — a lab trial on your actual substrate beats any spec sheet.

Cost and Sourcing Considerations

Manual guns start at a few hundred dollars, semi-automatic machines run into the thousands, and fully automatic lines scale with width and automation level. Beyond the sticker price, budget for:

  • Installation and commissioning — field and grounding must be set up correctly for the machine to perform.
  • Spare electrodes and wear parts — these wear and affect consistency over time.
  • Fiber recovery and filtration — good recovery can pay for itself in saved material.
  • Operator training — setup consistency is what keeps batch-to-batch quality stable.

Frequently Asked Questions

What voltage do I need for flocking?
Most flocking runs at 30–120 kV. Shorter, finer fibers work well at lower voltage, while long or dense fibers need the higher end of the range. Confirm the machine's range against your longest fiber before buying.
Manual gun or automatic line — which should I start with?
Start with a manual or semi-automatic setup for prototyping and small batches, then move to an automatic line once volume justifies it. The skills and settings you learn at small scale transfer directly.
Why is my flocking patchy or low-density?
Patchy coverage usually points to uneven grounding, a weak or unstable field, inconsistent fiber feed, or adhesive that has skinned over before the fibers land. Check grounding first — it's the most common culprit.
